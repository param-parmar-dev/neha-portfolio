Reporting Integrity Through a Data Platform Migration

Executive reporting built on spreadsheet logic that silently depended on upstream identifiers, naming conventions and refresh timing — and what it took to keep the numbers right while the data platform changed underneath them.

Root-cause diagnosis — OwnedSource logic redesign — OwnedValidation & regression testing — OwnedQuery specification — OwnedQuery development — Partnered (Platform/BI Team)Warehouse pipelines & lakehouse tables — Platform TeamDashboard delivery — Partnered
Executive Summary

Leadership steered the business off recurring dashboards spanning daily, weekly, monthly and trailing-twelve-month cadences, covering 15+ departments across multiple entities. The reporting logic sat in spreadsheets that resolved classifications by looking up identifiers against a central database, and that assumed source records would keep the same shape, the same names and the same refresh timing they had always had.

Over one reporting period the company began migrating master data to an MDM platform and moving reporting onto a warehouse and lakehouse layer. Four defects surfaced in quick succession — none caused by a formula anyone typed wrong, all caused by an upstream property changing. Every one produced numbers that looked entirely plausible in the output.

I owned the diagnosis, the source-logic redesign and the validation, and established the control that made the corrections stick: a reporting figure is not resolved until the platform owner who owns the underlying data has seen and validated the logic that produced it.

01Business problem

Dashboards that are consulted constantly and rebuilt rarely accumulate hidden dependencies. Classification logic in particular is fragile: when a system provides no flag for what something is, downstream logic has to infer it — by looking up an identifier, matching a name, or comparing a date. Each inference is a silent dependency on a source property nobody has agreed to keep stable.

A platform migration changes several of those properties at once. Identifiers move. Naming conventions get standardised. Refresh schedules start overlapping. The reporting output does not announce any of it — it just gets quieter about being wrong.

02My role

Root-cause diagnosis — Owned. Source logic redesign — Owned. Validation and regression testing — Owned. Query specification — Owned. Query development — Partnered with the platform / BI team, who own the warehouse and lakehouse tables. Dashboard delivery — Partnered.

03Approach & controls

Four defect classes, worked from symptom to root cause:

  • Hidden timestamp boundary: Period totals excluded every final-day transaction because source dates carried a time component the boundary logic did not account for. Corrected generically across periods, then tested for overlap and double-counting before rollout.
  • Identifier lookup break during master-data migration: With no system-level flag distinguishing new business from renewals, the logic classified a deal by looking up its ERP identifier against the central database and checking for a prior record in the same product category. When that database moved to the MDM platform the lookups broke, the helper columns stopped populating, and every renewal was silently reclassified as new. In the clearest month, a raw pull returning 13 deals resolved to 5 once renewals were correctly excluded.
  • Upstream naming-convention change: A standardisation applied at the system level severed the joins that downstream team-level reporting depended on. Isolated with the platform team and rebuilt on a source structure that did not depend on the old convention.
  • Automation colliding with warehouse loads: A scheduled script writing into sheets that also received pipeline uploads could execute concurrently with a load, producing stale, incomplete or blanked data. I disabled the affected automation and moved the process onto a pipeline-fed source before the issue reached reporting.

Controls established:

  • Scheduled refreshes alert on failure, so a job that does not run announces itself rather than quietly serving yesterday's data.
  • No automation writes to a source that a pipeline also loads. Ownership of a data surface is exclusive.
  • Fixes are made at the source, not in the helper column. A patched lookup survives until the next migration; a corrected source of truth does not need to.
  • Platform-owner validation gate: Where a correction depends on warehouse data, the tech owner reviews and validates the logic before the number is reported. This is not escalation — it is what allows a stakeholder to accept a restated figure without relitigating it.

The decision rule: if reporting and a business owner's own reconciliation disagree, the answer is not to defend either number — it is to put both on the same source.

06What I'd improve next

  • The defects that survive longest are the ones where reporting has to infer a classification the source system never recorded. A better lookup is a patch; a system-level flag is the fix.
  • Automation that a finance team builds during a platform gap should be designed to be retired. The measure of it working is that the platform makes it unnecessary.
  • Recommendation: treat every downstream dependency on an upstream property — identifier, naming convention, refresh window — as a documented contract with the data owner, so a change on their side arrives as a notification rather than as a variance nobody can explain.
